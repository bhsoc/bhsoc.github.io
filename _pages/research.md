My research examines how social structures and social judgments shape health across the life course. Across several interconnected lines of work, I study how health behaviors become objects of stigma, how gender and social relationships organize health practices, and how accumulated social inequalities contribute to aging and cognitive health. Methodologically, I combine experimental, longitudinal, statistical, and computational approaches to connect micro-level social processes with population patterns of inequality.

## Health Behaviors, Stigma, and Social Judgment

Why do some health behaviors become moralized while others are treated primarily as matters of personal preference? My research on health stigma examines how everyday health practices become bases of social judgment and exclusion. Using factorial survey experiments, I separate the effects of specific behaviors from the broader lifestyles in which they occur and examine how judgments vary across dimensions such as warmth, competence, devaluation, and social distance.

This work shows that health behaviors are not evaluated simply according to their medical consequences. Instead, people draw distinctions among behaviors according to perceived responsibility, norm violation, and broader cultural expectations about health. I use this research to develop a sociological account of how health becomes a moral and social classification system.

## Gender, Relationships, and Health

A second line of my research examines how health practices and outcomes are socially organized through gender and intimate relationships. Rather than treating gender only as a binary demographic characteristic, I study gender identity, gender presentation, and gendered behavior as related but distinct dimensions of social life.

My work examines how gender becomes embedded in health lifestyles, contraception, and spousal health influence. Across these projects, I am particularly interested in how health practices become ways of doing gender and how relationships simultaneously provide resources for health and reproduce inequalities in power, responsibility, and caregiving.

## Aging, Social Inequality, and Cognitive Health

My research on aging examines how inequalities accumulated across the life course shape cognitive health and social experiences in later life. Current work investigates educational disparities in Alzheimer's disease and related dementias and the ways family, social, and contextual resources contribute to cognitive functioning.

An emerging part of this agenda examines ageism as an interpersonal and relational process. I am interested in how age becomes intertwined with assumptions about health and cognitive functioning, how these judgments affect social inclusion and social connectedness, and how new technological environments may reproduce or modify age-based inequalities.

## Quantitative, Experimental, and Computational Sociology

My methodological work is motivated by substantive sociological questions. I develop and apply tools for interpreting categorical relationships, comparing effects across models and groups, and representing complex social processes in ways that remain substantively interpretable.

Across my research, I use factorial survey experiments, longitudinal and population-based data, latent-variable models, multilevel models, causal and predictive approaches, machine learning, and network-based methods. I view methodological innovation not as an end in itself, but as a means of making sociological explanations more precise and transparent.
